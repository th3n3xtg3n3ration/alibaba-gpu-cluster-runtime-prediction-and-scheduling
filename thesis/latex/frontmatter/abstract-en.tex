\begin{abstract}\label{chp:abstract}
\vspace{-1.5em}

A major challenge in large-scale GPU clusters is that schedulers cannot predict or control which heterogeneous, heavy-tailed jobs are assigned to shared hardware. Because of the long-tailed nature of workloads, GPU clusters experience extreme head-of-line blocking when a large ``elephant'' blocks many smaller ``mouse'' tasks. The traditional Predict-then-Optimize (PtO) framework attempts to solve this by minimizing point prediction errors (e.g., mean absolute error) before scheduling. In this thesis, we propose a decision-focused learning (DFL) approach to provide a new direction in machine learning. We show that ultimately, the objective of the predictor is not just to achieve accurate predictions on its own merit, but to optimize overall system-level performance measures, specifically Job Completion Time (JCT). Using data from Alibaba's Platform for Artificial Intelligence (PAI), we developed an end-to-end testbed tying runtime predictions to event-driven simulations across both small (32-GPU) and massive (256-GPU) cluster environments. All six models consume a novel sweep-line-based cluster-utilization feature computed at submission time. Results of the analysis show that a model's point-prediction accuracy (mean absolute error) does not reliably identify which policy delivers the largest scheduling gain. The categorical LSTM policy produced the largest JCT reduction over FIFO in our simulations at \emph{both} the 32-GPU and the 256-GPU scale -- ahead of every tree-based model despite its higher point-prediction error -- and the resulting job-completion-time ranking of all evaluated policies is nearly identical across the two cluster sizes (Spearman rank correlation above 0.98).\footnote{Exact JCT and rank-correlation figures are being refreshed following a code-level correction to the hyperparameter-tuning loss function and the addition of non-learned reference baselines (Per-User Median, Profile Median); see the Limitations section.} Although deep learning models produce higher point errors, they act as superior ``Learning to Rank'' engines protecting the ``mice'' from the ``elephants.'' These results provide concrete evidence that making the right relative ranking decisions matters more than achieving the right absolute prediction -- a conclusion that, on the present evidence, holds across cluster sizes rather than emerging only at the largest scale tested.

\end{abstract}
